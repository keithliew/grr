What Is a Guaranteed Rental Return (GRR)?

A plain-English guide: how the guarantee works, who pays it, what the law covers, what has gone wrong before, and what to ask. Last checked 9 November 2026.

In Short

  • What it is: a promise to pay you a fixed rent, usually a percentage of the price each year, for a set number of years after you buy a hotel suite, serviced apartment or resort unit.
  • Who pays: the developer or a company linked to it. It is a company's promise, not insurance and not a bank guarantee.
  • What comes after: usually a share of the hotel's profit, which goes up and down, or you take the unit back.
  • The big risk: the promise is only as strong as the company paying it and the agreement you sign.

How It Works: Guaranteed Years, Then Profit-Sharing Years

Most programmes have two stages. Only the first is promised.

Stage 1

Guaranteed years

A fixed rent for a set number of years, written into a rental agreement. It is usually paid every month or every three months, sometimes after running costs are taken off.

Stage 2

Profit-sharing years

Owners share part of the hotel's profit. Any figure for these years is the developer's estimate, not a promise.

Who Pays, and What Backs the Guarantee

  • The payer is named in the rental agreement. It may be the developer, a management company or a subsidiary. Check which company signs, and look it up.[1][4]
  • Nothing outside the company backs it. The rent comes from the payer's own money and the hotel's income. If the company runs short, the rent can stop.
  • The cost may be in the price. A developer can build the cost of the guarantee into the selling price. Compare the price per square foot with similar finished units nearby.[2]
  • Read the exit clauses. Agreements can let the payer stop in some events. For example, the draft Royal Lexis agreement lets the developer pause rent during events it cannot control, and rent stops if the unit is auctioned or sold without consent.[8]

The Legal Position in Malaysia

This is general information, not legal advice. Your agreement and your lawyer decide your position.

The guaranteed rent is a separate agreement from the purchase. To confirm

Your sale and purchase agreement (SPA) is covered by the Housing Development (Control and Licensing) Act 1966 when the project falls under it. The rental agreement that carries the guarantee is a separate contract, and the Act does not cover it.[2][3]

Disputes about the rent usually go to the civil courts, not the Homebuyer Tribunal. To confirm

The Tribunal for Homebuyer Claims hears claims under the housing law. A claim for unpaid guaranteed rent is a claim under the rental agreement, so it is normally brought as a civil case.[2][3]

Only what is written in the signed agreement counts. To confirm

A "guarantee" in a brochure, an advert or a sales talk does not by itself give you rights you can enforce. Your rights come from the agreement you sign.[1]

The company that pays may not be the developer. To confirm

The rental agreement is often with a management company or a subsidiary linked to the developer. If that company has little money, a court judgment may be hard to collect.[1][4]

Some schemes may fall under the Interest Schemes Act 2016. To confirm

A group representing owners has argued that some guaranteed-return property schemes meet the definition of an "interest scheme" under section 4(2) of the Act, which the Companies Commission of Malaysia (SSM) oversees. We found no SSM ruling on this.[6][7]

What If the Developer Cannot Pay? To confirm

  1. Check your agreement for who must pay, when, and what happens if they don't.
  2. Ask in writing, then send a formal letter of demand through a lawyer.
  3. Act with other owners. Owners have brought group civil claims for unpaid guaranteed rent.[4][5]
  4. Know the limits. Winning in court does not guarantee payment if the company has no money.[1]

What Has Happened When Guarantees Failed

The Arc @ Cyberjaya

137 owners said guaranteed rent payments stopped from 2016. They sued the developer together; the courts ordered RM3.97 million in unpaid rent and damages, and the Court of Appeal dismissed the developer's appeal in May 2018.[5][4]

A lawyer's view

A lawyer told The Edge that defaults on GRR schemes are "not unusual" and that several projects he had seen ended up in court.[4]

A complaint to SSM (2025)

A group representing 69 owners, with purchases of about RM46 million, said promised returns were not paid and the guarantee terms were not in their sale agreements. These are claims, not court findings.[6]

Questions to Ask Before You Buy

  1. Which company pays the guaranteed rent? Is it the developer itself, and how long has it been paying owners in its other projects?
  2. Is the guarantee written into an agreement I sign? Ask for the draft agreement before you pay a booking fee.
  3. For how many years, and from when? Does rent start at handover or only once the hotel opens?
  4. Is the rate before or after running costs, and is it on the full price or the discounted price?
  5. When can the payer stop paying? Look for clauses on crises, selling the unit, and loan defaults.
  6. What happens after the guarantee? Ask for the profit-sharing terms and what similar hotels actually earned.
  7. Can I leave the programme, use the unit, or sell it? What notice is needed?

Questions People Ask

What does GRR stand for?

Guaranteed rental return. The developer, or a company linked to it, promises to pay you a fixed rent for a set number of years after you buy, whatever the property actually earns.

Is a guaranteed rental return really guaranteed?

Only as far as the signed agreement says, and only as long as the company paying it can pay. It is a promise from a company, not insurance and not a bank guarantee.

What happens when the guaranteed years end?

Usually your rent switches to a share of the hotel's or operator's profit, which can be higher or lower, or you leave the programme and use, rent out or sell the unit yourself.

Who do I complain to if the rent is not paid?

Start with the company named in your rental agreement. If it still does not pay, buyers have taken civil action, sometimes together as a group. A lawyer can tell you what your agreement allows.

No. We explain how these programmes work, show each developer's figures with the source and date, and list the risks. Get independent legal and financial advice before you buy.
